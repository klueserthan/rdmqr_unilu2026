When we can’t flip a coin

Before the break: many treatments can’t be randomized

Age, gender, democracy, a university degree


Observational data: we see who got the treatment, but we didn’t assign it


✋ What is the main danger when we compare treated and untreated?

Time: 16:00-16:03 (3 min). Session plan (90 min): lecture and interaction 16:00-16:52 (52 min, about 6 min buffer built in) · student presentation and discussion 16:53-17:27 · wrap-up 17:27-17:30

  • Bridge from session 8: the “why we can’t just flip a coin” list (ethics, can’t be randomized, cost, noncompliance, external validity) and the treatments students said they could not randomize in the pairs exercise. Read two or three from the board
  • Answer to the question: selection bias. People choose (or are chosen for) the treatment for reasons that also affect the outcome
  • KW pp. 93-94: in an observational study the researcher does not control the values of X; observational studies try to emulate experiments and face the same causal hurdles
  • Today: two ways to deal with selection bias without our own coin flip

Is a top university worth it?

Graduates of expensive private US universities earn more than graduates of public ones

✋ Is that the effect of the university? Who goes there?

Time: 16:03-16:07 (4 min)

  • AJ ch. 2 opens with this question (pp. 47-50): is an elite private college worth the much higher tuition? Anecdotes of students who turned down Harvard for a public university (pp. 49-50)
  • Collect answers on the board: higher test scores, richer parents, more ambition, better networks. All of them would raise earnings anyway: selection bias, as with health insurance in session 8
  • Ask: could we randomize who goes to Harvard? (No.) So what can we do?
  • Swiss angle if useful: ETH vs. a smaller university, or a private business school; same logic

Two routes

Route 1: find a lottery someone else ran (natural experiment)

  • Oregon 2008: health insurance lottery (session 8)
  • Vietnam 1969: birth dates drawn to decide who was drafted
  • Also: thresholds, as-if random events (weather, disasters)

Route 2: compare like with like (control)

✋ Which route is open for the university question?

Time: 16:07-16:11 (4 min)

  • Natural experiments: the researcher does not assign the treatment, but something as good as random does. The comparison then works like an experiment
  • Oregon: AJ section 1.2 (pp. 24-29). Vietnam: Erikson & Stoker (2011, APSR 105(2)), “Caught in the draft”: the 1969 lottery assigned numbers to birth dates; men with low numbers (likely to be drafted) became more antiwar, more liberal and more Democratic than men whose high numbers protected them. Political science example; no need to quote numbers
  • Thresholds and as-if random events come back as regression discontinuity and other designs in later applied readings; do not go into detail
  • Gerber & Green count naturally occurring randomizations as experiments; designs that rely on argument rather than randomization (close elections) do not (GG pp. 13-17, from session 8)
  • Answer: no lottery for Harvard places. So route 2: control. The rest of today is about route 2

Compare like with like

All students

Split by test score

Hypothetical numbers

✋ Where did the gap go?

Time: 16:11-16:16 (5 min)

  • Hypothetical numbers; say so. Overall, private graduates earn about 84k, public graduates about 66k: an 18k gap
  • Split by test score, the gap is only 1k in every group. The overall gap comes from who goes where: 60% of private students have high scores, but only 15% of public students (low: 10% vs. 50%; middle: 30% vs. 35%)
  • Answer to the question: the gap was selection. Comparing only students with similar scores (like with like) removes it
  • Ask: is the test score enough? What else differs between private and public students within a score group (family income, ambition)? That is why AJ compares students who applied to and were admitted by the same colleges
  • AJ pp. 51-55, “the college matching matrix” (Table 2.1): nine made-up students grouped by the colleges they applied to and were admitted to (idea from Dale & Krueger). Naive gap 19,500; within group A −5,000, within group B +30,000; average 12,500. Only groups with both private and public students count
  • AJ’s point: regression does exactly this, automatically, as a weighted average of within-group comparisons (“make me a match, run me a regression”, p. 55). Regression mechanics: sessions 12-13
  • Apples to apples, oranges to oranges (AJ p. 55)

The private premium shrinks

Earnings in 1995 of students who started college in 1976 (Dale & Krueger; AJ, Table 2.2)

✋ Guess before each step: where does it end?

Time: 16:16-16:20 (4 min)

  • AJ pp. 59-64, Table 2.2 (p. 63), 5,583 students from the College & Beyond data, matched into 151 groups by the selectivity of the colleges they applied to and were admitted to (Barron’s categories)
  • Coefficients on private school, log earnings: .135 (no controls), .095 (+ own SAT), .086 (+ log parental income and demographics), .007 / .003 / .013 with selectivity-group dummies; read log points as roughly percent. With the selectivity groups the premium is not statistically significant
  • Students start in 1976, earnings in 1995 (AJ p. 51)
  • Table 2.3 (p. 67) shows the same with a larger sample (14,238) and a simpler “self-revelation” control: the average SAT of the colleges a student applied to, plus the number of applications. Premium falls from .212 to about .03
  • Message: most of the raw premium is selection. Who applies where tells us a lot about ambition and ability

Good control: a confounder

A common cause of treatment and outcome → control for it

✋ Which other confounders did we find for the university question?

Time: 16:20-16:23 (3 min)

  • DAGs as in session 3 (slide 12, “DAG 3: Confounding”), now with names. The red box marks the variable we control for
  • Cinelli, Forney & Pearl (2024), Model 1: controlling for a common cause blocks the “back door” path private college ← family background → earnings
  • Rich parents make private college more likely and raise earnings through many other channels (contacts, wealth). Comparing students with similar family background removes that part of the gap
  • Back to the board list from 16:03: test scores, ambition, parental income. AJ’s selectivity groups are a way to capture ambition and ability that we cannot measure directly

Bad control: a mediator

A step on the way from treatment to outcome

✋ What happens to the private premium if we control for the first job?

Time: 16:23-16:26 (3 min)

  • Cinelli et al., Model 11 (overcontrol bias): controlling for a mediator removes part of the effect we want to measure
  • If a private college helps mainly through better first jobs and networks, comparing students with the same first job hides that benefit: the premium shrinks, but not because it was selection
  • Rule of thumb: do not control for things that happen after the treatment and could be caused by it (“post-treatment” variables)
  • Session 3 called this mediation (DAG 2). Useful if you want to know how X works, harmful if you want to know whether it works
  • Comes back in the applied reading: are regional income and unemployment confounders or mediators of dialect distance?

Bad control: a collider

Suppose talent and looks are unrelated, and fame needs one or the other

✋ Among famous actors: are the good-looking ones more or less talented?

Time: 16:26-16:29 (3 min)

  • Hypothetical; say so. Talent and looks are assumed unrelated in the population; you become a famous actor through talent, looks, or both
  • Among famous actors, someone without great looks must be very talented, and someone less talented must look very good. So within the famous group, looks and talent are negatively related, although they are unrelated overall
  • Controlling for (or selecting on) a common effect of two variables creates a relationship that is not there: collider bias (Cinelli et al., Models 16-17; their example is the birth-weight paradox)
  • Same logic as session 6’s bonus example (admitted master students: grades and test scores), there called selection. Ask whether anyone remembers it
  • Confounder: arrows point out of the control. Collider: arrows point into it. Mediator: one in, one out

What you leave out

A left-out variable biases the estimate only if it…

  1. affects the outcome, and
  2. differs between treated and untreated


Check: does the estimate survive different sets of controls? (robustness)

✋ What could still be missing in the college comparison?

Time: 16:29-16:33 (4 min)

  • AJ section 2.3 (pp. 68-79): omitted variables bias (OVB), “short” regression (without the variable) vs. “long” regression (with it)
  • OVB formula in words (AJ pp. 71-72): effect of X in short = effect of X in long + (effect of omitted on Y in long) × (relationship of omitted with X). Both parts must be non-zero for bias
  • AJ Table 2.5 (p. 77): own SAT is strongly related to private school without the selection controls (1.165) but hardly at all with them (.066). That is why adding SAT barely changes the estimate once we compare like with like: .212 − .152 ≈ 1.165 × .051 (pp. 77-78)
  • AJ calls the OVB formula “the Prime Directive of applied econometrics” (p. 77)
  • Regression sensitivity analysis (AJ p. 75): if adding controls barely moves the estimate once key controls are in, we are more confident. Table 2.2 columns 4-6 stay near zero
  • Possible answers: ambition and motivation (partly captured by where you apply), family contacts, field of study, region. AJ’s family-size example (pp. 73-75)
  • KW pp. 92-99 (additional): observational studies come in two flavours, cross-sectional (many units, one time) and time-series (one unit, many times). KW’s “major difficulty” (p. 98): we must control for all confounders and can never be sure we have; conclusions stay tentative

Time: 16:33-16:43 (6 min in pairs, 4 min: two or three pairs share)

  • Builds on seminar questions 9.1 (DAG with the two confounders from session 8) and 9.2 (one control and why)
  • Typical problems to look for: arrows in only one direction (a “confounder” that only affects the outcome), a control that is caused by the treatment (mediator), confounders that are really the same thing measured twice
  • For prompt 3: the direction of bias follows the two parts of the OVB rule (does the missing variable raise or lower the outcome? is it more common among the treated?)
