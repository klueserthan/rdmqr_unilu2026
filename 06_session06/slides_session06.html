Correlation: what is it good for?

Variables are correlated when they tend to move together

Older people are more likely to vote

✋ What can we do with this fact?

  • Describe: the young are underrepresented (session 5)

  • Predict: which doors should a campaign knock on? (session 7)

  • Explain: does getting older make people vote? (today)

Time: 14:19-14:25 (6 min)

  • BF ch. 2: correlation = the extent to which two features of the world tend to occur together (p. 13); three uses: description, forecasting, causal inference (p. 19). “Older people vote more than younger people” is one of BF’s example statements (p. 19)
  • Description (BF pp. 20-21): age cohorts and turnout in the 2014 US congressional election (Fig. 2.4, p. 20); younger people were underrepresented
  • Look at the figure: turnout rises until about 70, then falls. The straight line misses this; a bridge to the next slide (what r does not tell you)
  • Forecasting (BF pp. 21-22): a campaign does not need to knock on doors of people who will vote anyway; age helps predict who they are, no causal story needed
  • Ask first: “What can we do with this fact?” Collect answers, then reveal the three uses
  • Stay on the third one: does ageing itself make people vote? Let students propose alternatives: older people differ in other ways (settled in one place, own a home, more routine); or it is a generation, not age (cohort). These are the four patterns coming up at 14:30
  • If asked: covariance = average product of deviations from the means, r = Cov(X,Y)/(sX sY), runs from −1 to 1 (BF p. 28)

What r does not tell you

Is \(r \approx 0\) evidence that two variables are unrelated?

Time: 14:25-14:28 (3 min)

  • BF “Straight talk about linearity” (pp. 30-31): all these measures capture linear relationships only; a zero correlation can hide a U-shape (Fig. 2.5). r runs from -1 to 1 but says nothing about the size of the relationship (p. 29)

  • Top row: the three textbook cases. Bottom row: strong relationships the linear correlation misses (U-shape, wave) and a strong correlation that still hides a non-linear shape (ceiling: steep rise, then flat)

  • Answer: no. r measures how well a straight line fits; always plot the data first (session 5)

  • Simulated data, not real

Ice cream and drownings

Does ice cream make people drown? If not, why do these lines move together?

Time: 14:28-14:30 (2 min)

  • Let them explain it: summer heat drives both ice cream sales and swimming (a confounder)
  • Then ask: are there other reasons why two variables could move together without one causing the other? Use the board list from the attendance question

Why correlation is not causation

When \(X\) and \(Y\) are correlated, maybe…

  1. Confounding: something else drives both
  1. Reverse causation: \(Y\) drives \(X\)
  1. Selection: looking only at a selected group creates the pattern
  1. Chance: in a small sample, patterns appear by accident

… and only then: \(X\) causes \(Y\)

Time: 14:30-14:34 (4 min)

Reveal one at a time; for each, ask “which reason on the board is this?” and give the attendance version.

  • Confounding: a third factor causes both X and Y. Attendance: motivated students attend more and study more. Ice cream: summer heat. BF’s own example: motivated students both take calculus and finish college (pp. 23-24)
  • Reverse causation: Y causes X. Attendance: students who do well enjoy the seminar and therefore keep coming; students who are lost stop coming
  • Selection: we only see a filtered group, and the filter creates the pattern. Easiest example: “old buildings are sturdier than new ones” — the badly built old ones were torn down long ago; we only see the survivors (comes back in the exercise)
  • Chance: with few cases, patterns appear by accident. Attendance: in one seminar of 24, three strong students who happen to attend a lot are enough
  • Only when we can rule these out does “X causes Y” become plausible; how to rule them out is sessions 8-10

What else could explain it?

In pairs (6 min): suppose a study finds…

A. Cities with more police officers have more crime

B. Countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel prizes

C. Buildings from the 1800s are sturdier than buildings from the 1980s

D. (Bonus) Among students admitted to a selective master’s programme, those with better grades have worse test scores

Which of the four patterns fits each finding?

Time: 14:34-14:44 (6 min in pairs, 4 min debrief)

  • All three are posed as hypotheticals (“suppose”); do not present them as findings of real studies
  • A: reverse causation (cities with more crime hire more police; BF p. 19 uses this as an example of a correlation); also confounding (big cities have more of both)
  • B: confounding (rich countries consume more chocolate and fund more research). Some students may know the published chocolate-Nobel correlation (Messerli 2012, NEJM); do not quote numbers from memory
  • C: selection (survivorship). Badly built old buildings collapsed or were torn down long ago; the 1800s buildings we see today are the ones that were built well. Builders 150 years ago were not better on average
  • D (bonus, for fast pairs; debrief only if time): selection. Suppose admission needs grade + test score above a threshold. A student with a weak grade only gets in with a strong test score, and one with a weak test score only with a strong grade; weak-weak applicants are rejected. So among the admitted, weak grades go with strong scores and vice versa. Nobody claims that good grades cause bad scores; the comparison is only within the admitted group. Simulation (admit the top 20% on grade + score): a correlation of 0 among all applicants becomes about −0.6 among the admitted, 0.3 becomes about −0.4, 0.5 becomes about −0.2; only a strong 0.7 stays positive (about 0.1). Selection pushes the correlation down
  • Debrief: one pair per finding; ask the others whether they found another explanation

Causation

“A causal effect is a change in some feature of the world that would result from a change to some other feature of the world.” (Bueno de Mesquita & Fowler 2021, p. 37)


Not “what is the cause of \(Y\)?”, but “does \(X\) change \(Y\)?”

Time: 14:44-14:47 (3 min)

  • BF p. 37 (quote; same definition in the text, p. 38); counterfactual thought experiments and the word “would” (p. 39); “What is the cause?” with World War I (pp. 42-43): ask whether something had an effect, not what the cause was

  • Social phenomena have many causes; we ask about the effect of one cause, not for the full list of causes of an outcome

  • Key word in the definition: “would”. A causal claim is about a world we do not see

Two potential outcomes

Does studying more improve your exam result?

  • \(Y_i(1)\): your result if you study a lot
  • \(Y_i(0)\): your result if you do not

Your causal effect: \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\)

Time: 14:47-14:50 (3 min)

  • BF p. 39: potential outcomes Y1i and Y0i, individual effect = Y1i − Y0i (Body Vibes example, Table 3.1)

  • Every student has two potential outcomes, one for each version of the treatment; the effect is the difference

  • Ask: for you personally, which of the two outcomes will you observe at the next exam?

The fundamental problem

We never see both outcomes for the same person

You either studied a lot or you didn’t. The other result is a counterfactual

✋ What would you need to observe to know whether studying more helped you?

Time: 14:50-14:53 (3 min)

  • BF p. 41: the fundamental problem of causal inference, named with the college-and-income example; summary p. 49

  • Let them try: “take the exam twice” (but you learn from the first try, and you are a different person a week later); “compare with my friend” (but your friend is not you)

  • Every answer is a substitute for the missing outcome; the question is how good the substitute is. That leads to the next slide

  • If asked for the formal version: we observe Yi = Di·Yi(1) + (1 − Di)·Yi(0), where Di = 1 if treated

Which comparison would you trust?

Does a tutoring course improve exam grades?


A. The same students’ grades before vs. after the course

B. Students who chose the course vs. those who didn’t

C. A coin flip decides who gets a place in the course


✋ Rank them. What could go wrong with each?

Time: 14:53-14:59 (6 min)

  • Vote first (which would you trust most?), then ask for the problem with each
  • A: anything else that changed over time (more experience, an easier exam): a before/after comparison mixes the course with time
  • B: selection and confounding: motivated students choose the course and would have done better anyway
  • C: the coin flip makes the two groups alike on average in everything except the course; the “didn’t get a place” group stands in for the missing counterfactual
  • Each design approximates the counterfactual differently: experiments (C) in session 8, large-N observational comparisons (B) in session 9, small-N comparisons in session 10; advanced designs in the applied readings of sessions 11-13
  • BF pp. 41-42: individual effects are invisible, so compare the average outcomes of groups (FDA randomized drug trial); averages of individual effects in the flu-shot example, pp. 45-46. BF ch. 3 does not use the term “average treatment effect”

Time: 14:59-15:01 (2 min)

  • Close the loop with the board list from 14:15
