The Logic of Scientific Research

Session 02

Research Design and Methods in Quantitative Research

Dr. K. Jonathan Klüser

The goal of good research


To “produce valid inferences about social and political life” (King, Keohane, & Verba 1994, p. 3)


What is inference?


The process of drawing conclusions from what we observe to what we do not (yet) observe.

  • Quote: KKV p. 3 (ch. 1, “The Science in Social Science”); basic reading today, pp. 3-33
  • Inference = using what we observe to learn about what we do not observe (KKV p. 8)
    • Descriptive: from observed facts to unobserved facts (e.g., a survey sample -> the population)
    • Causal: from data to causal effects (e.g., does canvassing raise turnout?)
  • Collecting facts alone is not science: the step beyond the data is (KKV p. 8)
  • Links to session 1 digression (inference vs. prediction) and to sessions 5-7 (descriptive, causal, predictive inference)

Scientific inference


Inferences that draw on evidence and reasoning, following a series of rules (or methods)


The methods make the science, not the content

  • They must be public
  • They must provide a measure of uncertainty
    (scientific conclusions are always tentative!)

KKV’s four characteristics of scientific research (pp. 7-9)

  1. The goal is inference: go beyond the particular observations collected
  2. The procedures are public: explicit, codified methods, so others can judge and replicate them (Merton: “science … is public, not private”)
  3. The conclusions are uncertain: inference without an uncertainty estimate is “uninterpretable”
  4. The content is the method: science is defined by its rules, not its subject matter (Pearson 1892: “The unity of all science consists alone in its method”)
  • Implication (p. 9): science is a social enterprise; work does not have to be beyond criticism to contribute

The research question

There is no logical recipe for having good ideas.

It depends on interest, creativity, knowledge… and luck.

However, some guidelines:

  • Must be relevant
  • Answering it should make a contribution
    (know your literature! or goal — e.g., private company)
  • Should be researchable (realistic plans to answer it!)
  • Should be focused
  • “No logical recipe”: KKV p. 14, quoting Popper: “there is no such thing as a logical method of having new ideas”
  • KKV’s two criteria (p. 15), also in seminar question 1:
    1. Important in the real world: consequential for political, social or economic life
    2. A specific contribution to an identifiable scholarly literature
  • Six ways to contribute (KKV pp. 16-17): (1) test an important hypothesis nobody has studied systematically; (2) challenge an accepted hypothesis; (3) help resolve a controversy; (4) examine unquestioned assumptions; (5) show an important topic has been overlooked; (6) apply theories or evidence from one literature to another
  • Personal motivation is fine but not a justification: “no one cares what we think - the scholarly community only cares what we can demonstrate” (p. 15)
  • Researchable: KKV p. 18: a topic that cannot be refined into a specific project permitting valid inference “should be modified along the way or abandoned”
  • Focused is our addition: narrow enough to answer with one design
  • Private-sector aside: there the “contribution” is to the organisation’s goal, not a literature

Types of research questions


Question Focus Example
What Descriptive What is voter turnout in country X?
How Explanatory How do campaigns influence turnout?
Why Causal Why do some groups vote more than others?


  • What/How/Why maps roughly onto the inferential goals of sessions 5-7: descriptive (what), mechanisms (how), causal (why)
  • Descriptive questions are legitimate research questions (session 5 readings: Gerring 2012, Mere Description; de Kadt & Grzymala-Busse 2025)
