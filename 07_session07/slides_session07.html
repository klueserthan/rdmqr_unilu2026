Before the break: causal inference


A causal effect compares two potential outcomes: \(\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\)

We never see both → we need a good counterfactual


A correlation can come from confounding, reverse causation, selection or chance

Umbrella or rain dance?

Which of these decisions needs a causal answer, and which only a good prediction?


A. Should I take an umbrella today?

B. Should the government fund rain dances to end a drought?

C. Which doors should a campaign knock on? (session 6: older people vote more)

D. Does knocking on doors raise turnout?

Time: 16:17-16:22 (5 min)

  • From Kleinberg, Ludwig, Mullainathan & Obermeyer (2015), AER 105(5): the umbrella only needs a prediction (will it rain?), the rain dance needs a causal effect (does dancing cause rain?). The paper writes the decision’s payoff as depending on a causal term and a prediction term; drop one and you get a pure case
  • Seminar question 7.1 asked exactly this; ask one or two students for their answer afterwards
  • Answers: A prediction; B causal; C mostly prediction (whom to skip because they will vote anyway, BF pp. 21-22, the age-turnout figure from session 6); D causal (the effect of canvassing)
  • C and D look alike but ask different questions: C needs to know who will vote, D needs to know what the knock changes

Predictive inference


Estimate outcomes we do not observe: the future, new cases, unlabelled data


Success = accurate predictions for new cases

not interpretable coefficients, not causal effects

Time: 16:22-16:25 (3 min)

  • Kleinberg et al.: only the prediction Ŷ enters the decision, so what matters is low prediction error, not unbiased coefficients. That is why machine learning (e.g. LASSO) can beat OLS here: it accepts some bias for less variance
  • “New cases” is the key: accuracy is judged out of sample (data the model did not see), not on the data it was fitted to (Hofman et al. 2021, p. 182)
  • Do not go into the ML details; Grimmer, Roberts & Stewart (2021) is the additional reading for that

Who should get a hip replacement?

Surgery is futile if the patient dies within a year anyway (Kleinberg et al. 2015)

  • Predict: who will die within a year of surgery?
  • For whom: older patients in the US Medicare system
  • Decision: whom to operate on, where to spend the money instead

✋ Which variables would you use? Do they need to be causes? What could go wrong?

Time: 16:25-16:30 (5 min)

  • Kleinberg et al. (2015), author manuscript pp. 4-5 and Table 1: Medicare claims for hip and knee replacements in 2010; predict death in months 1-12 after surgery (month 1 is excluded so the surgery’s own risk does not count). LASSO on about 3,300 claims variables; trained on one part of the data, evaluated on the rest
  • Numbers if asked (manuscript pp. 4-5): about 500,000 replacements a year at about $15,000 each; 4.2% of patients die within months 1-12. Avoid the “56%” figure for the riskiest 1%: the manuscript text and its Table 1 disagree
  • Variables: any that predict death (age, previous hospital stays, diagnoses), whether or not they cause it
  • What could go wrong: patients who would have lived get no surgery (false positives); the data only contain patients who were operated on (selection, from session 6); fairness if the predictors correlate with income or race
  • Other prediction problems in the paper: bail decisions, which restaurants to inspect, teacher hiring, creditworthiness

The four patterns are fine now


  • Ice cream sales predict drownings → staff more lifeguards (confounding)

  • Umbrella sales in the morning predict rain in the afternoon (reverse causation)

  • Age predicts turnout, whatever the reason (session 6)


✋ Why is a bad cause a good predictor?

Time: 16:30-16:35 (5 min)

  • This is seminar question 7.2 (good predictor, bad cause); ask one or two students for their example
  • Answer: prediction only needs the correlation to hold, not to know where it comes from. The four patterns from session 6 were problems for causal claims; for prediction they are information
  • Ice cream sales would be a fine predictor for a lifeguard service, and a useless lever: banning ice cream saves nobody
  • Bridge: but the correlation has to keep holding. Next slide

When predictions break


A prediction assumes tomorrow looks like yesterday


✋ A campaign stops knocking on the doors of older voters. Does its turnout model still work next election?


  • Google Flu Trends predicted flu from searches, but last week’s flu cases predicted just as well (Hofman et al. 2021)

  • Always compare with a simple baseline

Time: 16:35-16:39 (4 min)

  • Hofman et al. (2021), p. 182: predictive models assume stable relationships and can break when the situation changes, especially under interventions
  • Campaign answer: perhaps not. If older voters stop being contacted, their turnout may fall; the model learned a world that the campaign has now changed. Prediction is safe for “what will happen if nothing changes”, not for “what if we act”
  • Google Flu (Hofman p. 182): search-based predictions looked impressive, but a simple model using case counts from the previous weeks did about as well. Lesson: a prediction is only as good as the simplest alternative it beats
  • Hofman p. 182 also: choices in how accuracy is evaluated can make the same model look very good or rather poor

Explanation or prediction?

Social scientists want to explain: estimate the effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) (session 6)

Computer scientists want to predict: get \(Y\) right for new cases (today)

✋ Can a study explain well but predict badly? And the other way round?


Hofman et al. (2021): good research should be judged on both

Time: 16:39-16:43 (4 min)

  • Hofman et al. (2021), pp. 181-182: the two traditions value different things. Social science: causal effects estimated in sample, judged by whether the effect is significant. Computer science: accuracy out of sample. In practice the two are often confused
  • Explain well, predict badly: an effect can be clearly non-zero but small; a model with a significant coefficient may still predict very little of the outcome (Hofman pp. 182-183: a significance test is a weak test)
  • Predict well, explain nothing: the hip-replacement model or Google Flu; it tells you who, not why, and can break when the world changes
  • Today’s applied reading sits in between: it predicts the outcomes of experiments, but does it explain anything? Leave this for the discussion after the presentation

Prediction as a tool


Often prediction is not the goal but a step

  • Measurement: a model predicts labels for millions of texts (topic, tone, ideology) → then we describe or explain

  • Design: predict which experiments are worth running (today’s applied reading)


✋ Where could prediction help in your project?

Time: 16:43-16:46 (3 min)

  • Grimmer, Roberts & Stewart (2021), Fig. 1, p. 396: four tasks for machine learning in social science: discovery, measurement, causal inference, prediction. Their “agnostic approach” (p. 404): no single true model; pick the method that works best for the task and validate it (“doubt our models but trust our validations”)
  • Text classification: the task is predictive, the goal is measurement, which then feeds description or explanation
  • Grimmer et al. sec. 6 (pp. 411-413): causal inference is not “just another prediction problem”, because the counterfactual is never observed, so we cannot check the answer
