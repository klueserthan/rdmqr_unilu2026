Descriptive inference

The goal of good research is to “produce valid inferences about social and political life” (King, Keohane, & Verba 1994, p. 3)

What is descriptive inference?

→ To infer features (including associations, but non-causal relationships) of a broader population

Description is not “mere” description: we cannot explain what we have not described (KKV, p. 34)

Time: 16:17-16:21 (4 min)

  • Quote: KKV p. 3 (also session 2)
  • KKV p. 55: “Descriptive inference is the process of understanding an unobserved phenomenon on the basis of a set of observations”
  • KKV p. 34: description (collecting facts) is not yet descriptive inference; the inference is the step from observed to unobserved facts, and separating what is systematic from what is nonsystematic
  • KKV p. 34 against “mere” description: “We cannot construct meaningful causal explanations without good description”; it is not description vs. explanation that makes research scientific, but systematic inference by valid procedures
  • Associations count as description as long as we do not claim that one variable causes the other (de Kadt & Grzymala-Busse’s ladder, later)

Two main goals


  • Infer descriptive information about one variable

  • Infer descriptive information about two or more variables

Time: 16:21-16:22 (1 min)

  • One variable: what are typical values, how spread out are they? (KW 6.1, p. 125); this is most of KW ch. 6 and of the R demo
  • Two or more variables: associations and conditional associations; the last plot of the demo
  • KW p. 130: we need to know each variable’s variation before we can assess covariation (causal hurdle 3) or confounding (hurdle 4)

Variable types

Which types of variables do you know?

Can you give an example of each?

  • Categorical: values differ, but cannot be ranked
    → in our data: gender

  • Ordinal: values can be ranked, but the distances between them are not equal
    → in our data: none. Your example?

  • Continuous: equal distances; a one-unit increase always means the same
    → in our data: name length

Time: 16:24-16:29 (5 min)

  • Ask first which types students know (collect answers), then click 1 shows KW’s three types with definitions and the question “Can you give an example of each?”; collect one or two student examples per type before revealing ours (clicks 2-4)
  • KW 6.2 (pp. 126-130), the variable’s “measurement metric”:
    • Categorical (p. 127): cases are the same or different, no ranking. KW: religious identification (Catholic, Muslim, nonreligious, …)
    • Ordinal (pp. 127-128): rankable, but no equal unit differences. KW: retrospective family financial situation (“much better” … “much worse”); party ID (Republican, Independent, Democrat) only if Independents really lie between the two parties
    • Continuous (p. 129): equal unit differences. KW: age in years; one more year means the same at 21 and at 55
  • Good student examples for ordinal: education level, agreement scales (“strongly disagree” … “strongly agree”), interest in politics (not at all … very)
  • If asked: KW also call continuous variables “interval”; with a meaningful zero (like name length) “ratio” (p. 129, fn. 4). Not needed today
  • KW p. 130: in practice we must treat every variable as either categorical or continuous. Ordinal variables are the hard case: treat them as categorical and you throw information away; treat them as continuous and you assume equal distances. Check whether results change under the other choice

Central tendency

Which measures of central tendency do you know?


Categorical Ordinal Continuous
Mode: most frequent value ✓ ✓ ✓
Median: middle case when ranked ✓ ✓
Mean: sum of values / n ✓

Time: 16:35-16:39 (4 min)

  • Ask first, collect answers on the board (usually: mean, median, maybe mode), then click to show the table
  • Logic of the table: each measure needs a property of the variable. The mode only needs categories; the median needs a ranking; the mean needs equal distances (KW 6.2)
  • KW p. 131: the mode is the only measure of central tendency appropriate for a categorical variable
  • Ordinal: the median is fine; a mean of an agreement scale assumes equal distances, i.e. treats it as continuous (KW p. 130). Common in practice, but a choice to be aware of
  • Next two slides: the code and our results, first for gender, then for name length

Time: 16:40-16:42 (2 min)

  • Median (KW 6.4.1, p. 133): the value of the case at the exact centre when cases are ranked; with an even number of cases, the average of the two middle ones (KW’s incumbent vote: 51.4575)
  • Mean (KW 6.4.2, p. 136): sum of all values divided by n. Two properties (pp. 136-137): zero-sum (deviations from the mean add up to 0) and least squares (the mean minimises the sum of squared deviations), which is why it is the “expected value”, our best guess for a single case
  • Ask: why can the median name length be 16.5 letters, although nobody’s name has half a letter? With an even number of cases (24 students) there is no single middle case, so the median is the average of the 12th and 13th ranked values (16 and 17 letters with the current class list). Same rule as KW’s incumbent vote example (p. 133)
  • Mean vs. median: the median is robust to outliers, the mean is not. Ask: what happens to each if one student had a 60-letter name?

Spread

Which measures of spread (dispersion) do you know?

Categorical Ordinal Continuous
Range: max − min ✓
Q1, Q3: values at 25% and 75% of the ranked cases ✓ ✓
IQR: Q3 − Q1, the middle 50% ✓
Variance: mean squared distance from the mean ✓
Standard deviation: √variance, in original units ✓

Time: 16:42-16:45 (3 min)

  • Same logic as for central tendency: ask first, then click to show the table
  • Quartiles only need a ranking, so they work for ordinal variables (e.g., “a quarter answered ‘disagree’ or lower”); range, IQR, variance and SD subtract values, so they need equal distances
  • Categorical variables have none of these: their “spread” is the frequency distribution itself (bar plot, frequency table)
  • For ordinal variables you can still report the lowest and highest category, just not their difference
  • Next slide: the code and our results for name length

Dispersion (spread measures)

library(dplyr)

# Dispersion measures (name length)
data |>
  summarise(
    variance = var(Name_Length),
    sd = sd(Name_Length),
    range = max(Name_Length) - min(Name_Length),
    q1 = quantile(Name_Length, 0.25, type = 2),
    q3 = quantile(Name_Length, 0.75, type = 2),
    iqr = IQR(Name_Length, type = 2)
  )
# A tibble: 1 × 6
  variance    sd range    q1    q3   iqr
     <dbl> <dbl> <int> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1     22.3  4.72    20  12.5    20   7.5

Time: 16:45-16:47 (2 min). If behind schedule: show the result only, skip the code walkthrough

  • Rank statistics (KW 6.4.1, p. 134): quartiles Q1 (25%) and Q3 (75%); IQR = Q3 - Q1, the spread of the middle 50%; range = max - min
  • Moments (KW 6.4.2, p. 137): variance = sum of squared deviations from the mean, divided by n - 1 (degrees of freedom, KW fn. 8, more in session 11); standard deviation = its square root, back in the original units (“roughly … the average difference” from the mean)
  • Squaring keeps positive and negative deviations from cancelling out (p. 137)
  • type = 2 = KW’s hand method (p. 134): if the quartile falls between two ranked cases, average them, so Q1 here is the mean of the 6th and 7th of 24 names. R’s default (type 7) interpolates instead and would give 12.75. Different software, different quartiles: the method is a choice, so state it
  • Transition: keep these numbers in mind (median from the previous slide, Q1, Q3, IQR); the next plot draws exactly them

Summary of descriptive measures

Measure Formula Meaning Based on
Central tendency Mode — Most frequent value Frequencies
Median — Middle observation Ranks
Mean \(\bar{X} = \frac{\sum X_i}{n}\) Average value 1st moment
Spread IQR \(Q_3 - Q_1\) Middle 50% range Ranks
Variance \(s^2 = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2}{n - 1}\) Spread (squared units) 2nd moment
Std. deviation \(s = \sqrt{s^2}\) Spread (original units) 2nd moment
Shape Skewness \(g_1 = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^3 / n}{s^3}\) Asymmetry 3rd moment
Kurtosis \(g_2 = \frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^4 / n}{s^4}\) Peakedness 4th moment

Time: 16:49-16:51 (2 min). If behind schedule: 30 seconds, point to the grouping and move on

  • Three questions about a distribution: where is its centre, how spread out is it, what shape does it have?
  • Two ways of computing (KW p. 133; Stata’s summarize, detail in Fig. 6.3 shows both): rank statistics (median, quartiles, IQR) rank the cases and read off positions; moments are based on deviations from the mean, raised to the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th power. The mode is neither: it just counts
  • Rank statistics are robust to outliers; moments use every value and are pulled by extreme ones
  • Skewness (KW p. 137, fn. 10): 0 = symmetric; negative = longer tail to the left, positive = longer tail to the right
  • Kurtosis: how peaked the distribution is; the normal distribution has skewness 0 and kurtosis 3 (session 11)
  • Base R has no skewness/kurtosis functions; packages such as moments or e1071 do (not needed for the take-home exercises)

Good descriptive inference


Good estimators are:

  • Unbiased: right on average, no systematic error
    (session 4: valid, low bias)

  • Efficient: little spread across repeated samples
    (session 4: reliable, low variance)

  • Consistent: closer to the true value as n grows
    (session 4: the “more data” simulation)

→ Ultimately, how useful is your description for your theory?

Time: 16:55-16:58 (3 min)

  • KKV 2.7 (pp. 63-74): three criteria for judging methods of inference, used again for causal inference in KKV ch. 3
  • Session 4 was about measures (one measurement of one case); these are properties of estimators, the procedures that turn a sample into an estimate (e.g., the sample mean as an estimate of the population mean). Same logic, one level up
  • Unbiased (KKV p. 63): applied again and again, the estimator gives the right answer on average; bias = systematic error that shifts estimates in one direction (KKV: leaders inflating conflict for the researcher; corrupt vote counters)
  • Efficient (KKV p. 66): among unbiased estimators, the one with the smaller variance across hypothetical replications; KKV accept a little bias for a large gain in efficiency. 25 communities beat one “typical” community
  • Consistent (KKV p. 67): as n grows, the estimator’s variability shrinks to zero and it converges on the true value. Session 4’s simulation: the left panel is consistent; the right panel also settles, but on the wrong value
  • KKV fn. 14: an estimator can be unbiased but inconsistent (use only the first observation, however large n) or biased but consistent (the bias shrinks as n grows)
  • Last line: bridge to the next slides; description is judged by what it does for theory

The ladder of research questions

Source: de Kadt & Grzymala-Busse 2025

Time: 17:00-17:02 (2 min)

  • de Kadt & Grzymala-Busse (2025), “Good Description”, working paper (May 2025 version), Fig. 1 (p. 8): the same ladder for any theory A -> B
  • Map the previous slide’s questions onto the rungs: concept (what is economic performance?) -> characteristic (how often do incumbents lose?) -> association (bad years and losses together?) -> conditional association (still, given other factors?) -> causal effect -> explanatory mechanism (how?)
  • The upper four rungs are descriptive; only the bottom two are causal (session 6)
  • Not a ranking of importance, but you cannot answer a lower rung without being able to answer the higher ones (p. 8): no causal claim without knowing what A and B are and how often they occur. Session 4’s measurement problem is the top rung
  • Good descriptive questions (p. 11) reflect theory, build theory (describe phenomena that need explaining) or evaluate theory (check non-causal implications)
  • Further reading if students ask why description has a bad name: Gerring (2012), Mere Description (additional reading)
  • Seminar question 5 asked for exactly this: a descriptive question useful for your theory
