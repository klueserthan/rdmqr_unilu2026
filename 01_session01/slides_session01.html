Is it enough with collecting data and having the power to analyze it?


No! Science is all about design!

  • The goal is to make inferences about the world on the basis of empirical information

  • To make the right inferences, methods and rules are crucial

  • This course is about these rules and methods, and how to apply them, with a focus on quantitative research

Possible digression: inference vs. prediction

  • Inference: learn how the world works. Estimate a quantity (a mean, a causal effect) and state how uncertain it is. Example: does canvassing increase turnout, and by how much?
  • Prediction: forecast outcomes for new cases as accurately as possible. The model may be a black box. Example: which citizens will turn out?
  • A model that predicts well need not reveal causes, and a credible causal estimate need not predict well.
  • The course returns to this in sessions 5–7 (descriptive, causal and predictive inference).
  • Syllabus learning outcome 1: differentiate the main targets of scientific inference

Let’s dig deeper!

The presentation

  • 25 minutes max.

  • Two parts: paper summary/critique and research proposal

  • The summary/critique should focus on the research design and its limitations

  • The research proposal should build on these limitations

    • The papers will be good, so build on them rather than (try to) destroy them

  • After the presentations, there will be a 20 minutes Q&A

  • Fills the second part of a session: 25 min presentation + about 20 min discussion
  • Always the applied reading of that session
  • Build on the paper, don’t tear it down
  • Details: syllabus sections “Present the applied reading” and “How to Make a Good Presentation”

Checklist Part I – Summary (~10 min)

  • Core ideas (1–2 slides, ~3 min): Summarize the argument and main takeaways.
  • Research design (~4 min): Present the research question, hypotheses, method, data type, unit of analysis, and key findings.
  • Critique (~3 min): Identify strengths and main limitations of the research design (measures, validity, scope, etc.). Use clear communication: diagrams, step-by-step reasoning, examples, visual aids.
  • Rough split: 3 min core ideas · 4 min design · 3 min critique
  • Rubric link: understanding and critique quality
  • Design elements to name: research question, hypotheses, method, data type, unit of analysis, findings

Checklist Part II – Proposal (~15 min)

  • Address one main limitation: Focus on one key issue and justify why it matters.
  • Propose one solution or extension: Suggest a feasible new design, replication, different data, or alternative method.
  • Be realistic: Assume research funds are available, but do not “change the world” (e.g., deciding election dates).
  • Seek guidance if needed: Contact the lecturer if unsure about preparation.
  • Longest part of the presentation
  • Rubric link: reasoning (one key limitation, why it matters) and feasibility
  • Realistic: funds yes, but no deciding election dates or invented countries

Additional Information Checklist

  • Evaluation: graded with the rubric on the next slide.
  • Avoid pitfalls:
    • Don’t overcrowd slides.
    • Avoid superficial content.
    • Manage time (25 min total).
    • Prepare early, rehearse, and refine slides.
    • Practice in front of a friend and ask for feedback.

How your presentation is graded

Each criterion earns 0–5 points. 15 points (half the maximum) = 4.0 (pass), 30 points = 6.0. Pairs receive the same grade. The full conversion table is in the syllabus.

Criterion Full points when you…
Clarity Follow the two-part structure, use uncluttered slides with visual aids, and stay within 25 minutes
Understanding Present the argument, research question, hypotheses, method, data, unit of analysis and findings accurately
Critique quality Name specific strengths and the main limitations of the research design
Reasoning Focus on one key limitation, explain why it matters, and derive your proposal from it
Feasibility Propose a concrete, realistic design that addresses the limitation
Discussion Answer questions accurately and use the feedback to improve your proposal
  • 6 criteria × 0–5 points = 30; 15 points = 4.0, 30 = 6.0, linear, rounded to half grades; below 15 at most 3.5
  • Pairs get the same grade
  • Full conversion table: syllabus, Grading rubric section; printed sheet in 00_grading
  • The presentation is 40% of the course grade
