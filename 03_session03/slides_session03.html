What is a theory?


A tentative conjecture of how a phenomenon of the world works


Either implicitly or explicitly, it conveys a set of causal relationships between concepts

  • Definition: KW p. 3: “a tentative conjecture about the causes of some phenomenon of interest”
  • Concepts are abstract (e.g., “political trust”); variables are their measurable counterparts (session 4: measurement)
  • KW p. 4, Figure 1.1: theory -> hypotheses -> empirical test -> evaluate hypotheses -> evaluate theory -> scientific knowledge (shown again at the end)

Some social science examples


  • Rational Choice Theory (Downs)
    Individuals vote to maximize utility

  • Partisan Identification Theory (Campbell et al.)
    Partisanship as a “perceptual screen”

  • Modernization Theory (Lipset)
    Economic development fosters democracy

  • Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner)
    Group membership leads to group bias

  • Reveal one theory at a time: the name appears first, the one-line claim on the next click
  • For each name ask: “Who has heard of this? What does it claim? What is X, what is Y?”, then reveal the claim

Background

  • Rational choice: Downs, An Economic Theory of Democracy (1957). Voters maximise expected utility; leads to the “paradox of voting” (KW 2.7.2, “The Puzzle of Turnout”, pp. 45-47)
  • Party identification: Campbell, Converse, Miller & Stokes, The American Voter (1960). Partisanship as a long-term “perceptual screen” that filters information
  • Modernization: Lipset, “Some Social Requisites of Democracy”, APSR (1959). Economic development -> democracy
  • Social identity: Tajfel & Turner (1979). Group membership -> in-group favouritism and out-group bias; today’s applied reading (Turnbull-Dugarte & Wagner 2025) also deals with political identities

Good theories should be…


  • Causal (explain why something happens)

  • Non-normative (does not prescribe what ought to happen)

  • General (work across contexts)

  • Parsimonious (explain much with little)

  • KW “rules of the road”, ch. 1.5 (pp. 17-20): focus on causality; don’t let data alone drive your theories; consider only empirical evidence; check your ideology at the door and avoid normative statements; pursue both generality and parsimony
  • Slide shows four of the five; the missing one (“don’t let data alone drive your theories”) comes up on the next slide
  • Generality and parsimony pull against each other: a more general theory usually needs more conditions (KW p. 20)
  • KKV p. 20 on parsimony: “Simple theories have higher prior probabilities” (Jeffreys); a judgement about the world, not a rule

Bad examples to contrast with each criterion

  • Not causal: “Populist parties are successful in Europe” (a description, no why); “Countries with more universities are more democratic” (an association with no mechanism)
  • Normative: “Switzerland should lower the voting age to 16 because young people deserve a voice” (says what ought to be, not what is)
  • Not general: “Brexit happened because of the Leave campaign’s bus slogan” (explains one case only; no claim about other referendums)
  • Not parsimonious: “Turnout depends on the weather, income, age, education, candidate charisma, TV ads, the day of the week …” (a list that explains everything and therefore nothing)
  • Bonus, violating “don’t let data alone drive your theories” (KW 1.5.2): correlate 200 variables with turnout and write a theory around whichever one is significant

From ideas to good theories

Again, no magic formula!


But some strategies:

  • Is it relevant? (starting with the RQ)
  • Is there interesting variation? (e.g., puzzles)
  • Is it rooted in knowledge of the world? (e.g., specific events)
    • Know local, think global: can you drop the proper nouns (names of people, places, dates)?
    • “Why did the SVP gain in 2023?” ⟶ “Why do radical right parties gain when immigration is salient?”
  • Does it build on the literature? (e.g., gaps, extensions, implications)

KW ch. 2 strategies (basic reading, pp. 25-52)

  • “No magic formula”: KW’s lightning analogy (pp. 25-26); you can’t guarantee being struck, but you can climb a hill in a thunderstorm
  • Relevant: promising theories answer interesting research questions (KW 2.2)
  • Interesting variation (KW 2.3): look at the dependent variable; cross-sectional (military spending across countries, Fig. 2.1) vs. time-series (US presidential approval 1995-2005, Fig. 2.2)
    • Caveat: examine variation in Y only; developing theory after seeing X and Y move together violates “don’t let data alone drive your theories” (p. 30)
  • Knowledge of the world (KW 2.4):
    • From a specific event to a general theory: 9/11 rally -> Mueller’s (1973) theory of rallies around the flag (KW 2.4.1)
    • Drop the proper nouns (KW 2.4.2, pp. 32-33): “Physicists don’t have theories that apply only in France, and neither should we.” Proper nouns = names of people, countries and, KW add, specific dates. If your theory still makes sense once you replace “Mitterrand”, “France” or “1986” with general concepts (president, country, period of divided control), it is general; if it collapses, you only have an account of one case.
    • KW’s example: Lewis-Beck (1997), “Who’s the Chef?”: French cohabitation 1986-88 (socialist president Mitterrand, Gaullist prime minister Chirac) -> general theory: under divided control, voters hold whoever controls the economy (there: the prime minister) accountable for economic outcomes
    • SVP example on the slide is ours: from one party and one election to a claim about a class of parties and a condition (issue salience)
  • Build on the literature (KW 2.5-2.6): three strategies: a new Y; an existing Y with a new X; a new Z that modifies an established X -> Y. Also: critically examine previous research; “someone already did it” is not the end (2.6.2)

From theories to models


A model is a simplified representation of reality


“All models are wrong, but some are useful.”
— George E. P. Box

  • Theory = the general account; model = a simplified version focused on specific concepts and relationships
  • Box quote: George E. P. Box, statistician; the phrase appears in Box & Draper (1987)
  • Next slide: the Lucerne transport map as a model
  • Map analogy from KW p. 16, quoting James Rogers (2006): a street map distorts reality, and “it is precisely because the map distorts reality … that it is a useful tool”
  • Ask: what does this map leave out? (distances, streets, terrain) Why is it still useful? (it answers one question: how to get from A to B)
  • KW p. 16: models are “often more useful to us when they are inaccurate”; glaring failures point to new theories

When are models useful?


In a research design context, models are useful because they bridge the gap between theory and hypotheses


Models are a simplified version of a theory that focus on specific concepts and relationships


By removing complexity, they help us make theoretical claims testable (and thus falsifiable)

⟶ More later on hypotheses

  • Models make theories testable by choosing which concepts and relationships to keep
  • Falsifiability (KKV pp. 19-20): choose theories that could be wrong; derive as many observable implications as possible; be as concrete as possible
  • “More later on hypotheses”: two slides ahead

From concepts to variables

Concepts are the abstract building blocks of a theory
e.g., economic performance, political trust, democracy

Variables are measurable representations of concepts: quantities that take two or more values
e.g., real GDP growth in %, trust on a 0–10 scale

From concept to variable = operationalization (session 4)

  • Dependent variable (DV): the outcome we want to explain
  • Independent variable (IV): the factor(s) we think explain the DV
  • Other variables: moderators, mediators, confounders…
  • Why concepts first: theories are stated in concepts; hypotheses and tests need variables. The move between them is measurement (operationalization), the topic of session 4 (KW ch. 5)
  • KW p. 7: political scientists “order their thoughts about the political world in terms of concepts that scientists call variables and causal relationships between variables”
  • KW p. 9 (Figure 1.2, “From theory to hypothesis”): at the theory level, variables “do not need to be explicitly defined”; economic performance is a concept ranging from very strong to very poor. At the hypothesis level we measure it, e.g., “one-year real economic growth per capita”
  • KW p. 3 (fn. 1): a variable is “a definable quantity that can take on two or more values” (e.g., turnout as % of eligible voters)
  • One concept, many possible variables: economic performance -> inflation, unemployment, real growth (KW p. 10); each choice gives a different hypothesis
  • DV = outcome (Y); IV = explanatory factor (X)
  • Moderator: changes the size or direction of X -> Y (e.g., candidate gender in the deepfake paper from session 2)
  • Mediator: the channel through which X affects Y
  • Confounder: a common cause of X and Y (next slide)

Models as variables and relationships

Examples with Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs):


1. Direct effect

\[X \longrightarrow Y\]

2. Mediation

\[X \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y\]

3. Confounding

\[ \begin{array}{ccc} & Z & \\ \swarrow & & \searrow \\ X & \longrightarrow & Y \end{array} \]

  • DAG 1: direct effect of X on Y
  • DAG 2: mediation, X affects Y through M (e.g., education -> political knowledge -> turnout)
  • DAG 3: confounding, Z causes both X and Y; the naive X-Y association is biased unless we account for Z (e.g., income -> education and income -> turnout)
  • Arrows = assumed causal direction; “acyclic” = no feedback loops
  • Returns in session 6 (causal inference) and session 9 (Cinelli et al. 2022, good and bad controls)

From models to hypotheses

Models specify relationships between variables; hypotheses translate them into testable claims


Example: the theory of economic voting (KW, pp. 8–10)

Theory: voters hold the government responsible for the economy, so they reward incumbents for good and punish them for bad economic performance

Model: Economic performance ⟶ Incumbent vote

Hypothesis: The higher real economic growth per capita in the year before an election, the higher the vote share of the incumbent party

  • KW’s running example in ch. 1 (pp. 8-10), so students have read it (KW ch. 1 is background to today’s ch. 2)
  • Theory = the causal explanation: why should X affect Y? KW p. 9: “because voters hold the president responsible for management of the national economy”
  • Model: two concepts and one arrow; everything else (candidates, campaigns, war) deliberately left out
  • Hypothesis: the concepts are now measured (economic performance -> one-year real growth per capita; incumbent vote -> incumbent party’s vote share); directional (positive)
  • Other hypotheses from the same theory: unemployment instead of growth; individual level (people who think the economy got worse are less likely to vote for the incumbent); other countries
  • Literature if asked: Key (1966), The Responsible Electorate; Fiorina (1981), Retrospective Voting in American National Elections
  • KW test it with US presidential elections: scatter of GDP change and incumbent-party vote share (KW ch. 8, Figs. 8.3-8.4)

Hypotheses


A hypothesis is a testable statement about the expected relationship between variables.

  • Non-directional hypothesis: only states there is a relationship, not its direction.
    • E.g. Income is related to voting turnout.
  • Directional hypothesis: specifies the direction of the relationship.
    • E.g. Higher income increases the likelihood of voting turnout.

Directional hypotheses can be positive (variables move in the same direction) or negative (variables move in opposite directions).

  • Non-directional example: income is related to turnout. Directional: higher income increases turnout (positive relationship)
  • Negative example: more distance to the polling station -> lower turnout
  • Directional hypotheses are more falsifiable (KKV p. 20: be concrete), so prefer them when theory allows
  • Null hypothesis (KW p. 4): no relationship; statistical testing in session 11
